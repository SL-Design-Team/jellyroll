Voice

Our UI voice is clear, calm, and helpful. People use SnapLogic to do complex, high-stakes integration work, so we get to the point, stay even when something goes wrong, and never make them feel at fault.

Clear and direct
Lead with what matters. One idea per sentence. Plain words over clever ones.
Select a Snaplex to run the pipeline
Calm and professional
No hype, no jokes, no exclamation points. Stay level when things go wrong.
Couldn’t save the pipeline
Helpful
Say what happened and what to do next. Never blame the person.
Enter a name to continue

Grammar and style

These rules follow the Microsoft Writing Style Guide. They make text quicker to scan, easier to translate, and easier for assistive technology to read aloud.

Use active voice
Name who or what does the action. Use passive only when the actor is unknown or doesn’t matter.
Write to “you”
Address the person as you and your. Never write the user in UI text. Use we for SnapLogic. Avoid I, me, and my in labels; a plain noun is usually better than My pipelines.
Use the present tense
Describe what happens, not what will happen. The pipeline runs when you select Run.
Use contractions
Don’t, can’t, you’re, and it’s keep text natural. Keep do not and cannot in warnings about irreversible actions and in Snap error messages, where the negative must be unmistakable and strings stay terse.
Lead with what matters
Put the key word or action first so it survives scanning. Start most statements with a verb, cut you can when it isn’t needed, and avoid there is and there are.
One idea per sentence
Aim for 20 words or fewer. A sentence with more than a couple of commas usually needs splitting. Replace long, complex sentences with a list or a table.
Write in plain language
Short, everyday words. No jargon unless it’s a term your audience already uses. Read it aloud: if it doesn’t sound natural when you say it to a colleague, rewrite it.
Keep a consistent reading level
Aim for roughly an eighth-grade reading level. WCAG 3.1.5 (AAA) sets lower secondary education as the ceiling for text that has no simpler alternative.
Use parallel structure
Start every item in a list or every sibling heading with the same kind of word, such as a verb or a singular noun.
Include that and articles
that, the, and a make sentence structure clear to readers and to translation software.
Avoid exclamation points
They add noise and read as emotion. Save the rare exception for a genuine celebration that your team has approved.

Inclusive language

Write so that everyone feels addressed. This follows Microsoft’s bias-free communication guidance.

Use gender-neutral language
Rewrite to use you, use a plural, or refer to a role such as owner or admin. If you can’t write around it, use the singular they. Never write he/she.
Put the person first when you mention disability
Write people who are blind or have low vision. Don’t mention disability unless it’s relevant, and avoid words that imply pity.
Choose neutral technical terms
Replace terms that carry unintended meaning. The Terminology page lists parent/child in place of master/slave.
Avoid slang, idioms, and culture-specific references
They confuse non-native readers and don’t translate. Say what you mean directly.
Be careful with examples
Use varied, realistic names in sample data, and don’t make generalizations about people, places, or cultures.

Writing for translation

Text that’s easy to read is easier to translate. Assume your strings will be localized, even if they aren’t yet.

Build complete strings
Don’t assemble a sentence from fragments in code. Word order and grammar change between languages, so give translators the whole sentence with placeholders.
Leave room to grow
Translated text is often longer than English, commonly by about 30 percent and by more for very short strings. Let containers grow instead of truncating.
Keep modifiers and clauses simple
Avoid chains of modifying words, avoid linking more than two clauses with and, or, or but, and keep only next to the word it limits.
Use one word per concept
Don’t switch between synonyms for the same feature, and don’t use one word for two things. Terminology lists the words we’ve settled on.
Use only common abbreviations
Spell out the rest on first use. Don’t put text in images.
